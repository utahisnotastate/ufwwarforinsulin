## Disclosure 10 — Alpha cells can become beta cells, and the immune system still finds them

**Explain it to a friend.** The pancreas has a second kind of cell sitting right next to the
insulin-making ones, doing a different job. They're close cousins. You can talk one into
becoming the other.

**Old school.** Beta cells destroyed in Type 1 diabetes are gone forever. The pancreas is a
closed system.

**This disclosure says.** It isn't. Alpha cells — which make glucagon — share a developmental
lineage with beta cells and are abundant and self-replenishing. Flip the right transcription
factors and they convert, in place, into glucose-sensing insulin secretors. No transplant, no
donor, no surgery.

**Status.** ✅ the conversion · ❌ the way the source assembles it · ❌ the cure claim ☠️

The conversion is real. Herrera's group showed it in mice after ablating >99% of beta cells,
and reprogrammed **human** donor alpha cells with PDX1 + MAFA to secrete insulin
glucose-responsively, reversing diabetes in mice for six months.

But the source's specific recipe — "transient FoxO1 ablation plus Pdx1/Ngn3/MafA" — is a
synthesis of three separate literatures in **three different cell types**, and no study ever
ran it. The FoxO1 work is in *gut* cells, and in beta cells it runs the opposite direction
(β→α, not α→β). The Ngn3 triad converts *acinar* cells.

**And the headline claim is contradicted by its own source paper.** The source says converted
cells are invisible to the autoimmune T cells that caused the disease. Furuyama 2019, Figure 5,
in a section the authors titled *"Insulin-secreting α-cells are hypoimmunogenic,"* reports:

> **"Insulin-producing α-cells were sensitive to PPI-directed CTLs, similar to β-cells."**

Which makes sense, and is the whole problem: the converted cell's job is to make
preproinsulin, and **preproinsulin is the main thing the immune system is attacking.** In
autoimmune NOD mice, the new cells worked for about **four months** and then diabetes came
back.

**Kid takeaway.** You can turn one kind of cell into another. The body's defense system is
still watching.

## Disclosure 11 — Stem-cell islets are curing people right now, and the price is immunosuppression

**Explain it to a friend.** Not a theory. Not mice. In 2025 a trial grew insulin-making cells
from stem cells, infused them into twelve people with Type 1 diabetes, and most of them stopped
needing insulin.

**Old school.** Islet transplants need donor pancreases, so supply caps the treatment at a few
hundred people.

**This disclosure says.** Grow the islets instead. Differentiate them from a stem cell line and
supply stops being the constraint.

**Status.** ✅ **VERIFIED**, with a cost the source never mentions.

**Zimislecel (VX-880)**, *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2025. Twelve participants at full
dose:

- **10 of 12 insulin-independent at one year**
- **All 12** free of severe hypoglycemic events, HbA1c under 7%, time-in-range over 70%

That is a functional cure, and it is the most important thing in this entire repository.

**The catch is the whole catch.** It requires standard immunosuppression. One participant died
of **cryptococcal meningitis** attributed to the regimen. It is still investigational — not
approved. And the companion programme that tried to avoid immunosuppression by encapsulating
the cells in a device, **VX-264, failed.**

So: the field has solved *making* the cells and has **not** solved protecting them. Which is
exactly the problem Disclosure 10's transdifferentiation claim pretends doesn't exist.

For context: **Lantidra**, the first donor-islet cell therapy, was FDA-approved in June 2023 —
also requiring lifelong immunosuppression, also available at essentially one center.

**Kid takeaway.** Scientists have cured a few people. The medicine that stops the body
rejecting the cure is still dangerous, and that's the next problem to solve.

## Disclosure 12 — You can delay Type 1 before it starts

**Explain it to a friend.** Type 1 diabetes doesn't begin the day someone gets sick. The immune
attack runs silently for months or years first. There is now a drug you can give during the
silent part that pushes the onset back by about two years.

**Old school.** You find out you have Type 1 when you get sick. Nothing can be done before
that.

**This disclosure says.** There are detectable stages. Stage 1 is autoantibodies with normal
glucose; stage 2 is autoantibodies plus abnormal glucose but no symptoms; stage 3 is clinical
diabetes. If you catch someone at stage 2, you can intervene.

**Status.** ✅ **VERIFIED** — and it is the first disease-modifying therapy in Type 1 diabetes.

**Teplizumab (Tzield)**, an anti-CD3 antibody. In 76 at-risk relatives, median time to clinical
diabetes was **48.4 months on drug vs 24.4 months on placebo** — roughly two extra years. FDA
approved November 2022 for stage 2, age 8 and up. The label was expanded **down to age 1 in
April 2026**, and in **June 2026** it received accelerated approval for preserving insulin
production in children newly diagnosed at stage 3.

It delays. It does not cure, and nobody claims it does.

**Why it belongs here.** The source's entire frame is that institutional medicine is
stagnant and the real answers are suppressed. Teplizumab is the counterexample: a genuinely
novel mechanism, in humans, approved, expanding — and arrived at by exactly the slow
screening-and-trials machinery the source dismisses. It also makes autoantibody **screening**
newly worth doing, which is a change in practice, not just a drug.

**Kid takeaway.** Doctors can now sometimes see Type 1 coming, and push it back.
