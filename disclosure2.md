## Disclosure 4 — The liver is supposed to get it first

**Explain it to a friend.** Your pancreas doesn't release insulin into your bloodstream
generally. It releases it into a private pipe that runs straight to the liver, and the liver
takes most of it before any reaches the rest of you. A syringe in your belly skips the private
pipe entirely.

**Old school.** Insulin is insulin. Get it into the blood and it works.

**This disclosure says.** Route matters. Native secretion goes into the **portal vein**, with
roughly 50–80% extracted by the liver on first pass, producing about a 3:1 portal-to-peripheral
gradient. Subcutaneous injection abolishes that gradient. To get enough insulin to the liver to
shut down its glucose production, every other tissue — coronary arteries, microcapillaries,
kidney — has to be exposed to far more insulin than nature ever delivered there.

**Status.** ✅ **VERIFIED** as physiology. ❌ **NOT TRUE** as a solved problem.

**The part the source leaves out, and it's the good part.** People built the fix. Lilly's
**insulin peglispro** was hepato-preferential and it *worked* — better HbA1c than glargine,
less nocturnal hypoglycemia, weight loss instead of weight gain. **It was discontinued in
December 2015** because it raised liver fat and liver enzymes. Diasome's HDV-insulin met
non-inferiority in 2019 but helped some patients and *harmed* others depending on their
starting control, and still hasn't begun Phase 3 seven years later.

**Zero hepato-preferential insulins are approved.** The physiology was right and the drug
still didn't work. [Details](docs/endocrinology/the-hyperinsulinemia-debate.md).

**Kid takeaway.** Where medicine goes in matters as much as what it is.

## Disclosure 5 — The molecule can be its own glucose sensor (in rats)

**Explain it to a friend.** Instead of a sensor and a pump and a computer, imagine insulin that
switches *itself* on when blood sugar rises and off when it falls. One injection, no machine,
no decisions.

**Old school.** You measure glucose, then you decide how much insulin to give.

**This disclosure says.** Hang a glucose-binding group off the insulin molecule at the B29
lysine. At low glucose it folds shut against a carrier and is inactive; glucose competes it
open and the insulin goes to work. The feedback loop lives in the chemistry.

**Status.** ✅ chemistry · ✅ animals · ❌ **humans** · ⛔ "hypoglycemia becomes impossible"

The chemistry is real and the B29 site is right. But **no glucose-responsive insulin has ever
worked in a human** — and here is the part that surprised us when we checked it properly:
**the boronate chemistry the source describes has never been given to a person at all.**

Merck spent over half a billion dollars on MK-2640, the only GRI with published human data —
and it wasn't a boronate. It used a mannose-receptor mechanism instead. In people with Type 1
diabetes it showed **no glucose-dependent change in clearance at all** and was 25× less potent
than regular insulin. Novo's NNC2215 (*Nature*, 2024) is a genuine advance — and across the
glucose range that actually occurs in a body, it's about a **3× modulator, not a switch.** Rats
and pigs only. Lilly bought Protomer in 2021 for up to ~$1bn to get into this race; still
nothing in humans.

So the claim isn't just unproven. **The proposed mechanism is two steps from any human
evidence**, and roughly a billion dollars has gone into the field without producing a single
successful human trial.

**And the "one shot lasts weeks" idea runs backwards.** The longest-acting insulins we have are
*weekly*, and in Type 1 diabetes they **increase** severe hypoglycemia — 10% vs 3% in QWINT-5.
A long-acting depot you can't recall is a risk, not a safety feature.

**Kid takeaway.** A really good idea that has not worked in a person yet. Both halves of that
sentence matter.

## Disclosure 6 — Oral insulin keeps failing, and the reason is instructive

**Explain it to a friend.** Insulin is a protein, and your stomach digests proteins. That's why
it's a shot. People have been trying to get around this for a century.

**Old school.** You can't swallow insulin. Stomach acid destroys it.

**This disclosure says.** You can, if you hide it. Grow proinsulin inside plant chloroplasts —
the plant cell wall shields it through the stomach, gut bacteria break the wall down in the
intestine, and a cholera-toxin-B tag carries it across the gut lining into the **portal vein**.
Oral delivery *and* liver-first targeting at once, grown in a greenhouse, no refrigeration.

**Status.** ✅ the plant expression · 🟡 what the mouse experiment showed · ❌ ever reaching
humans

Daniell's chloroplast work is real: proinsulin at **47% of total leaf protein** in tobacco,
53% in lettuce. But the famous mouse result was **oral tolerance induction preventing
autoimmune diabetes onset** — not glycemic control by absorbed insulin. Different drug,
different endpoint. It never entered human efficacy trials.

**And oral insulin generally has a graveyard.** Oramed's Phase 3 (n=710) **missed its primary
endpoint in January 2023** after a positive Phase 2b. Novo's oral insulin 338 actually
*matched* injected glargine on HbA1c in Phase 2 — and was killed because ~1–2% oral
bioavailability made the amount of drug per pill too expensive to manufacture.

**No oral insulin is approved anywhere.** And that last one is the most interesting failure in
this whole repository: **the science worked and the economics killed it**, which is precisely
the inverse of the source's thesis.

**Kid takeaway.** Sometimes a thing works in the lab and still doesn't make it to you — and
sometimes the reason is money, not biology.
