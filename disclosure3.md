## Disclosure 7 — The cell isn't broken. It's full.

**Explain it to a friend.**

> A freight car is packed to the roof with crates. The doors bulge. The yardmaster arrives with
> ten more crates, turns the key — nothing moves. He decides the key is broken and fetches a
> hydraulic ram.

The key works. The car is full.

**Old school.** In Type 2 diabetes, cells have lost the ability to respond to insulin.
Something has failed. Push harder.

**This disclosure says.** Nothing has failed. When fat accumulates inside muscle and liver
cells where it doesn't belong, particular lipid species build up near the membrane, switch on
an enzyme family called protein kinase C, and that enzyme **deliberately disables the insulin
signaling relay** by phosphorylating it at a site that jams it. The cell is refusing delivery,
on purpose, because letting more fuel in while already saturated would damage its
mitochondria.

**Status.** ✅ the mechanism · 🟡 the "it's protective, not a defect" framing

The biochemistry is mainstream — most associated with Gerald Shulman's group at Yale, and
demonstrated in **humans**, not just cells. The protective-adaptation reading is a real
published position (Hoehn, *PNAS* 2009; Nolan et al., *Diabetes* 2015), not a fringe one, but
it is contested, and the source overstates it by saying resistance is "**not** a breakdown" —
even its strongest advocates call it adaptive *and* pathological over time.

**Two corrections the source needs.** The liver arm doesn't run through IRS-1 any more — Shulman's
own lab moved it to the insulin receptor itself in 2016. And IRS-1 Ser307, which the source
lists as an inhibitory site, turned out to work the **opposite way** in knock-in mice.
[Details](docs/technical/insulin-receptor-and-signaling.md).

**Kid takeaway.** When a door won't open, check whether the room is already full before you
blame the key.

## Disclosure 8 — Muscle has a second door

**Explain it to a friend.** There are two ways to get sugar into a muscle. One needs insulin.
The other just needs the muscle to *work*. They're completely separate systems, and the second
one keeps working when the first one is jammed.

**Old school.** Insulin opens the door. No insulin, no glucose uptake.

**This disclosure says.** Contracting muscle triggers glucose uptake through a different
internal pathway entirely — driven by the energy stress of contraction and by calcium release,
converging on the same vesicle machinery from the other side. **No insulin receptor, no IRS-1,
no PI3K, no Akt required.**

**Status.** ✅ **VERIFIED** — this is real and genuinely under-taught. It is why exercise
lowers blood glucose, and why it keeps working in people with significant insulin resistance.

**Three small corrections:** the calcium-arm effector is CaMKII, not CaMKK; TBC1D1 and TBC1D4
are not interchangeable; and Rac1 is a required third limb the source omits.

**☠️ And one enormous one.** This pathway is insulin-**signaling**-independent. It is **not
insulin-independent.** It still needs a permissive amount of circulating insulin, because
without insulin the liver keeps dumping glucose and nothing restrains ketone production. A
person with Type 1 diabetes who exercises *instead of* dosing doesn't get euglycemia — their
glucose goes **up** and they head into ketoacidosis. The ADA position statement warns about
exactly this.

The phrase "non-insulin-dependent glucose disposal" is accurate about a pathway and lethal as
a treatment plan.

**Kid takeaway.** Moving your body helps your muscles use sugar. It is not a replacement for
medicine anyone needs.

## Disclosure 9 — The brake is real, and you still can't leave the glucose high

**Explain it to a friend.** Two things are true at once: high blood sugar in Type 2 diabetes is
a *symptom* of an overloaded system, and high blood sugar will still blind you.

**Old school.** High glucose is the disease. Lower it by whatever means.

**This disclosure says.** High glucose is overflow. The fire is upstream. Treating the number
without treating the overload is treating the smoke.

**Status.** 🟡 **PARTLY TRUE as mechanism** · ❌ **NOT TRUE as a conclusion** ☠️

This is where the source goes from provocative to dangerous. It concludes that insulin therapy
in Type 2 diabetes is "an iatrogenic disaster." The randomized evidence says otherwise:

| Trial | Result |
|---|---|
| **ORIGIN** (12,537 patients, 6.2 years) | **Neutral.** HR 1.04 |
| **UKPDS 10-year follow-up** | **Benefit** — all-cause death ↓13%, MI ↓15% |
| **ACCORD** | Harm from an aggressive *HbA1c target*, and the excess occurred in patients who **failed** to reach it — tracking disease severity, not insulin |

The "insulin kills" literature rests almost entirely on one observational cohort where insulin
was prescribed to sicker people. Randomized meta-analyses find mortality **RR 0.99**.

And one claim is flatly backwards: the source says exogenous insulin drives beta-cell
apoptosis. Short-term intensive insulin in newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes produces **~45–51%
one-year remission** versus ~27% on oral agents. Beta-cell *rest* preserves function.

**The defensible version.** Escalating insulin to force normal glucose in long-duration,
high-cardiovascular-risk Type 2 diabetes gives you hypoglycemia and weight gain without
cardiovascular benefit. That is why modern guidance puts metformin, GLP-1 agonists, and SGLT2
inhibitors ahead of insulin. It is an argument about **sequencing**, not about insulin being
poison — and the distance between those is where people end up in intensive care.

**Kid takeaway.** Understanding *why* something is happening doesn't mean you get to ignore it.
