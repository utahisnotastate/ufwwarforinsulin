# When a patient brings you this

**Audience:** primary care, emergency medicine, hospitalists, urgent care, pharmacists — any
clinician who is not an endocrinologist and has ten minutes.
**Purpose:** triage the clinical risk, know which of the patient's claims are actually correct,
and keep the patient.

---

## The one-minute version

A patient has arrived with a document — this one, or something like it — arguing that insulin
therapy is harmful, that insulin resistance is protective, that insulin can be manufactured at
home for pennies, and that a radio-frequency device can lower blood glucose without insulin.

**Immediate clinical questions, in order:**

1. **Type 1 or Type 2?** This determines whether you are managing a difference of opinion or an
   emergency in progress.
2. **Have they already changed or stopped anything?** Ask directly and without judgment. "Have
   you skipped or cut back any doses?" gets a better answer than "you haven't stopped your
   insulin, have you?"
3. **Are they using insulin they did not get from a pharmacy?**
4. **Symptoms now?** Polyuria, polydipsia, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, Kussmaul
   respiration, fruity breath, altered mental status.

**If Type 1 and they have reduced or stopped insulin: this is a DKA pathway, not a
conversation.** Check glucose, ketones (beta-hydroxybutyrate preferred), venous gas, and
electrolytes now. Euglycemic DKA is possible, particularly with SGLT2 inhibitors or reduced
carbohydrate intake — **a normal glucose does not exclude it.**

**If they are injecting non-pharmaceutical insulin:** treat as a potential source of sepsis,
pyrogenic reaction, injection-site infection, and unpredictable glycemia. Secure a
pharmaceutical supply the same day. Consider anti-insulin antibodies if glycemic control is
erratic and unexplained.

## What the document gets right

This section exists because of the clinical dynamic, not out of fairness. A patient who has
done a lot of reading and is told *"that's all nonsense"* learns that you didn't read it, and
takes their next question somewhere worse. Several of their claims are correct, and
acknowledging them specifically buys you the credibility to be believed on the part that
matters.

**They are right that insulin resistance has a lipid-driven mechanism, not a "broken
receptor."** Intramyocellular and hepatic diacylglycerol accumulation activating novel PKC
isoforms, with inhibitory serine phosphorylation of IRS-1, is mainstream biochemistry. If they
cite Shulman, they are citing real work.

**They are right that subcutaneous insulin is non-physiological.** Endogenous insulin reaches
the liver first with substantial first-pass extraction; subcutaneous delivery produces relative
peripheral hyperinsulinemia and relative hepatic underexposure. This is a genuine and
long-recognized limitation.

**They are right that C-peptide was dismissed as inert and isn't.** It has demonstrable
biological activity. (The trial outcome is where they go wrong — see below.)

**They are right that contraction-mediated glucose uptake is insulin-independent.** It is, and
it's clinically useful.

**They are right about the price.** Manufacturing cost is a small fraction of what was charged,
people rationed, and people died. This grievance is legitimate and it is usually the emotional
engine of the whole belief system. Do not dismiss it. Addressing it materially — see below — is
often the single most effective intervention available to you.

## What is wrong, and how wrong

| Claim | Reality | Clinical risk |
|---|---|---|
| A VLF/scalar device activates insulin receptors without insulin | No mechanism; no evidence; the arithmetic fails on its own terms | **Lethal in T1D.** DKA within 12–48h of insulin cessation |
| Insulin therapy is "an iatrogenic disaster" in T2D | Overstated. ORIGIN showed CV neutrality for basal insulin over ~6 years. The hyperinsulinemia debate is real; the verdict is not | Moderate — may drive non-adherence |
| Insulin resistance is protective, so don't lower glucose | Mechanistically defensible, therapeutically false. Glucotoxicity is route-independent | Moderate–high |
| C-peptide co-administration reverses microvascular disease | Phase 2b (Cebix, 2015) **failed** — no better than placebo. Program terminated | Low direct risk; high credibility cost if you don't know this |
| Home synthesis yields clinical-grade insulin for <$0.01 | Off by orders of magnitude; ignores potency assay, endotoxin, sterility, immunogenicity | **High** |
| Glucose-responsive insulin makes hypoglycemia "chemically impossible" | No GRI has succeeded in humans. Merck's MK-2640 did not deliver | Moderate — false security |
| Alpha-to-beta transdifferentiation cures T1D without immunosuppression | Real preclinical biology; the immune-evasion claim is not supported | Low direct risk |

The most useful single fact to have ready is **the C-peptide trial failure.** It is the one the
patient will not have, it is verifiable in thirty seconds, and it demonstrates that you engaged
with the strongest version of their argument rather than the weakest. It also teaches the
general lesson — mechanism is not outcome — without you having to lecture.

## Having the conversation

**Lead with the concession, not the correction.** "You're right that the subcutaneous route is
unphysiological, and you're right that the price was indefensible" costs you nothing and
changes the frame from confrontation to collaboration.

**Separate the two diseases explicitly.** Much of the source material's argument is about Type
2 pathophysiology, and much of its danger is to people with Type 1. Patients often don't
realize the document is conflating them. Saying so is clarifying rather than dismissive: *"a
lot of what's in here is about Type 2, and the argument is more reasonable there than you'd
think — but you have Type 1, and that argument doesn't transfer, here's why."*

**Be concrete about the timeline, not the theory.** Abstractions about ketoacidosis land less
well than: *"without insulin, you'd be in hospital within two days, and it's a bad two days."*

**Do not argue the physics.** You will lose, not because you're wrong but because you can't win
a technical argument on unfamiliar ground in a 12-minute appointment. Point them at
[the worked analysis](../technical/why-the-physics-layer-fails.md) and move on. What you *can*
say in one sentence: "the frequency they give doesn't even come out of their own equation."

**Give them something to do.** People adopt these frameworks partly out of agency. Offer a real
one: a CGM trial, a referral, a structured exercise plan — exercise is both genuinely effective
and consonant with their belief that non-pharmacological routes matter. Meeting them there is
clinically sound, not a concession.

**Address cost in the room, materially.** Ask what they're paying. Then act on it:

- The $35/month insulin cap for Medicare Part D beneficiaries
- Manufacturer patient-assistance programs from all three major makers
- Unbranded biologics and authorized generics at substantially lower cash price
- Community health center 340B pricing
- Walmart ReliOn human insulin as a low-cost fallback — with the caveat that NPH/regular require
  different timing and education than analogues, and switching someone over is a real clinical
  task, not a handoff

A patient who leaves with their insulin affordable is far less likely to return to a document
promising to make it for free.

## Red flags for escalation

- **Any** insulin reduction or omission in Type 1 → ketone testing today
- Self-manufactured or non-pharmaceutical insulin in use → stop immediately, replace, screen for
  infection
- Recurrent unexplained DKA → ask directly about intentional omission; consider diabulimia and
  psychiatric referral
- Rapidly deteriorating control with no dose explanation → consider anti-insulin antibodies,
  degraded or improperly stored product, or occult non-adherence
- Patient reports a device they are using *instead of* insulin → urgent endocrine involvement

---

## Documentation

Record: the specific beliefs stated, what the patient reports actually doing (as distinct from
believing), the risks you explained, the patient's response, and the plan. If the patient
declines recommended therapy, document capacity and the specific risks discussed. This is a
population at real risk of a preventable death, and the note matters.
