# C-peptide: the full story

← [Endocrinology index](README.md)

**Claims addressed:** A6, A7, A8.

This is the most compelling argument in the source material and the one most worth
understanding completely, because the shape of it — beautiful mechanism, decades of positive
preclinical work, one adequately powered trial — recurs throughout this repository.

---

## 1. The setup, which is genuinely good

Proinsulin is cleaved in the secretory granule by PC1/3 at Arg31-Arg32 and PC2 at Lys64-Arg65,
with carboxypeptidase E trimming the exposed basic residues. Insulin and the 31-residue
C-peptide are therefore co-secreted in **strict 1:1 molar ratio**.

Recombinant insulin contains none of it. So since 1982, every person with Type 1 diabetes has
received insulin stripped of the peptide that always accompanied it in nature — and C-peptide
was classified as inert connecting material on no evidence beyond the absence of a known
function.

That is a real and legitimate observation, and the source is right to find it remarkable.

## 2. The bioactivity is real

C-peptide is not inert:

- **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** — in human renal tubular cells, C-peptide raises Na,K-ATPase expression via
  PKC- and MAPK-dependent activation of the transcription factor ZEB.
- **eNOS** — increases eNOS mRNA and protein and NO release in aortic endothelial cells.
- **MAPK phosphorylation** — demonstrated in multiple cell systems.
- Review: Wahren J, Larsson C. *Diabetes Res Clin Pract* 2015;107(3):309–19.

Animal data are moderately supportive: Samnegård B et al., *Nephrol Dial Transplant*
2005;20(3):532–8 showed C-peptide **prevents** glomerular hypertrophy and mesangial matrix
expansion in diabetic rats. Note the verb — prevention of progression in young STZ rats, not
reversal of established lesions.

## 3. The receptor is not established

The source states C-peptide "binds specific G-protein-coupled membrane receptors." The
candidate was **GPR146**, from Yosten GLC, Kolar GR, Redlinger LJ, Samson WK, *J Endocrinol*
2013;218(2):B1–B8, where GPR146 knockdown blocked C-peptide-induced cFos. **The authors
themselves stated no direct physical interaction was demonstrated.**

Independent replication failed:

> Lindfors L et al. *"Is GPR146 really the receptor for proinsulin C-peptide?"*
> **Bioorg Med Chem Lett 2020;30(13):127208 (PMID 32354568)**

No intracellular response to C-peptide up to **33 µM** in two assay systems. No dynamic mass
redistribution in GPR146-overexpressing cells. No β-arrestin recruitment. No internalization by
fluorescence microscopy. Conclusion: *"Our results do not support GPR146 as the receptor for
C-peptide."*

A separate pharmacology thesis found no GPR146-specific signalling via Gαs, Gαi/o, Gαq/11,
Gα12/13, β-arrestin 2, or ERK1/2. Even the pro-GPR146 camp's own review (Kolar & Yosten,
*J Intern Med* 2017;282:4–16) concedes that radioligand binding, FRET/BRET, and co-IP evidence
for physical association remain to be done.

Add to this the frequently-reported **bell-shaped dose-response** — effect at 1 nM, absent at
10 nM — which is itself hard to reconcile with a conventional receptor mechanism and which
badly complicates any dosing strategy.

**Accurate phrasing:** C-peptide binds cell membranes with specificity and produces a
G-protein- and Ca²⁺-dependent signalling pattern, but the proposed receptor has failed
independent replication and direct binding has never been demonstrated.

## 4. The trial

> **Wahren J, Foyt H, Daniels M, Arezzo JC.** *"Long-Acting C-Peptide and Neuropathy in Type 1
> Diabetes: A 12-Month Clinical Trial."* **Diabetes Care 2016;39(4):596–602. PMID 26884473**

Cebix's "Ersatta" (CBX129801, pegylated C-peptide), Phase 2b:

| | |
|---|---|
| Design | Randomized, placebo-controlled, 32 sites (US/Canada/Sweden) |
| n | 250 |
| Population | T1D with mild-to-moderate peripheral neuropathy |
| Dose | 0.8 mg or 2.4 mg weekly vs placebo |
| Duration | 52 weeks |
| **Primary endpoint** | **Sural nerve conduction velocity** |

**Result:**

| Endpoint | C-peptide (pooled) | Placebo |
|---|---|---|
| **Sural NCV change** | **+1.0 ± 0.24 m/s** | **+1.2 ± 0.29 m/s** |
| mTCNS | unchanged | — |
| Vibration perception threshold | ~25% improvement | — |

**The placebo group did better on the primary endpoint.** The only positive signal was a single
secondary endpoint in a trial that missed both its primary and its clinical neuropathy score.

**Cebix dissolved in February 2015**, approximately 30 days after unblinding, having raised
~$50M and having been mid-raise on a further $35M. The CEO described drug and placebo as
"indistinguishable."

Two things make this result especially hard to argue around:

1. **The senior author is John Wahren**, the field's principal advocate and the person who did
   most of the foundational work. This is not a hostile or underpowered attempt by skeptics.
2. **The trial was adequately sized and long enough.** 250 patients over 52 weeks against a
   nerve conduction endpoint is a real test.

## 5. Why "primary etiology of microangiopathy" cannot be right

The source's strongest version — that C-peptide deprivation is *the direct, primary etiology*
of diabetic microangiopathy — is refuted by the single largest body of evidence in diabetes.

**In DCCT/EDIC, every participant had Type 1 diabetes. C-peptide was absent in both arms.** The
only variable was glycemic control. Complication rates diverged by 60–76%.

If C-peptide deficiency were the primary cause, intensive glycemic control should have done
little or nothing in a uniformly C-peptide-deficient population. It did a great deal. The
causal weight sits on hyperglycemia.

### ☠️ Why this specific claim is the most dangerous in Block A

It is causally displacing. A patient who believes microvascular disease is driven by C-peptide
deficiency rather than hyperglycemia has been given a reason to **relax glycemic control** —
and relaxing glycemic control in Type 1 diabetes is the most reliable way to produce exactly
the complications in question.

There is also a practical exposure: C-peptide is obtainable from research-chemical and
grey-market peptide suppliers. A document asserting that co-administration reverses
microangiopathy is an invitation to self-inject an unregulated peptide for an indication where
the one properly powered trial failed.

## 6. What remains genuinely open

The trial failing does not make C-peptide biologically inert, and it would be as much an
overcorrection to say so as it was to call it packing material.

Defensible open questions:

- Was the **endpoint** wrong? Sural NCV over 52 weeks may be insensitive to a slow
  microvascular effect.
- Was the **population** wrong? Established neuropathy may be past the point of rescue, whereas
  the animal data showed *prevention*. A primary-prevention trial in newly diagnosed patients
  has never been run.
- Was the **molecule** wrong? Pegylation alters distribution, and given the bell-shaped
  dose-response, the tested exposures may have sat on the wrong part of the curve.
- Human nephropathy data remain **four small studies, n = 74 total**, graded weak by
  meta-analysis (*PLoS One* 2015;10(5):e0127439). The larger renal trials were never done.

So the honest status is: **a real bioactive peptide, an unresolved receptor, a failed trial in
one indication, and an untested hypothesis in another — with no commercial sponsor remaining.**

That is a considerably less satisfying ending than the source offers. It is also what the
evidence says.
