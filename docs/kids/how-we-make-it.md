# How we make insulin

← [Back to: Insulin, for kids](README.md)

So people with Type 1 diabetes need insulin every day, for their whole lives. Millions of
people. Where does all of it *come from*?

The answer is genuinely one of the best stories in science, and it has three chapters.

---

## Chapter 1: We took it from pigs and cows

For about sixty years — from the 1920s to the 1980s — all insulin came from animals.

Slaughterhouses saved the pancreases from pigs and cattle and shipped them, frozen, to
factories. There the pancreases were ground up and the insulin was pulled out and cleaned.

It worked, and it saved millions of lives. But it had problems:

- **It took a LOT of animals.** Roughly **two tonnes** of pig pancreas to make eight ounces of
  insulin — enough for only a handful of people for a year.
- **Pig insulin isn't quite human insulin.** It's off by one building block. Cow insulin is off
  by three. Close enough to work, but some people's immune systems noticed and reacted badly.
- **If there weren't enough pigs, there wasn't enough insulin.** That's a frightening way to
  run a medicine supply.

## Chapter 2: We taught bacteria to make it

In 1978, a small company called Genentech did something that sounds like science fiction, and
in 1982 the first product of it went on sale.

They wrote out the human instructions for insulin — the gene — and put those instructions
**inside a bacterium**. *E. coli*, the same kind of bacteria that live in your gut right now.

And the bacteria just... made it. Human insulin. Perfectly human, not pig, not cow. Because the
instructions for building proteins are written in the same code for every living thing on
Earth. Bacteria, trees, mushrooms, you — same code. So a bacterium can read a human recipe just
fine.

This was the first medicine ever made this way, and it changed everything. No more pigs. Make
as much as you want.

> **Why this works at all:** every living thing uses the same genetic alphabet. Life only
> invented it once, about four billion years ago, and everything since has inherited it. When
> you put a human gene in a bacterium, the bacterium can read it because you are cousins.

## Chapter 3: We taught yeast to do it better

Here's the twist. Bacteria can *make* insulin, but they're not great at *finishing* it.

Insulin isn't a straight string. It's two short strings folded together and pinned with three
little clasps — chemical staples called **disulfide bonds**. They have to go in exactly the
right three places, or the insulin is just a useless tangle.

Bacteria don't do this folding step. They crank out the string and dump it in a heap inside
themselves — scientists call these heaps **inclusion bodies**. So the factory has to burst the
bacteria open, dissolve the heaps with harsh chemicals, and then very carefully coax the
strings into folding correctly. It works, but it's a lot of steps and you lose a lot along the
way.

**Yeast is better at this.** Yeast — the same thing that makes bread rise and ferments beer — is
a more complicated kind of cell, more like ours. It has an internal workshop (the
*endoplasmic reticulum*) with special helper proteins whose whole job is folding things
correctly and putting the staples in the right spots.

So if you give the recipe to yeast:

1. The yeast reads it and builds the insulin string.
2. Its internal workshop folds it properly and puts the three clasps in the right places.
3. And then — this is the great part — **the yeast pushes it out the door** into the liquid it's
   swimming in.

You don't have to break the yeast open at all. The insulin is just *in the soup*. You let the
yeast settle to the bottom, and pour off the top.

This is real. It isn't a theory. Novo Nordisk, one of the biggest insulin makers in the world,
has been using yeast to make insulin for decades.

---

## So why isn't insulin cheap?

Good question, and it's the honest one.

Making the insulin molecule is not the expensive part. Yeast will happily make it out of sugar
water. The expensive part is **proving it's safe enough to inject into a person.**

Think about what has to be true before that liquid can go in someone's body:

- **It has to be exactly the right strength.** Insulin is measured in "units." If a vial is
  stronger than the label says, someone takes too much and their blood sugar crashes
  dangerously. If it's weaker, they slowly get very sick. You can't eyeball this. You need
  real tests.
- **It has to be completely clean.** Not just "no yeast in it." Bacteria leave behind a nasty
  leftover chemical called **endotoxin** that causes raging fevers and shock if you inject it —
  and it's so small it goes straight through the filters that catch the bacteria themselves.
- **It has to be sterile and stay sterile** all the way into the vial, in rooms cleaner than an
  operating theater.
- **Every single batch has to be tested** and the paperwork kept.

That's what costs money, and that is *not* red tape for its own sake — people died learning
why each of those rules exists.

But here's the thing that *is* unfair: when researchers add it all up, actually producing a vial
of insulin — including all that safety work — costs only a few dollars. For many years people
in the United States were being charged hundreds. Some people couldn't afford it and tried to
make their vials last longer by taking less than they needed. **People died from that.**

That gap between a few dollars to make and hundreds of dollars to buy is not a science problem.
It's a people problem — about patents, companies, insurance, and law. Which means people can
fix it. Some of it has already been fixed: there are now caps that bring the price down to $35
a month for a lot of people in the US. That didn't happen by itself. People pushed.

---

## ⚠️ The part you must not skip

Because insulin is *simple to make* and *expensive to buy*, people sometimes get the idea that
they could just make their own at home.

**They can't, and trying is extremely dangerous.** Everything in the "why isn't it cheap"
section above is the reason. Yeast soup with insulin in it is not medicine. You can't know how
strong it is, you can't get the endotoxin out with a kitchen filter, and insulin that's even a
little bit folded wrong can make your body attack *all* insulin forever — including the real
kind that keeps you alive.

Grown-up scientists with real labs are working on making insulin cheaper and more available,
and that's great work. But it belongs in a lab with real testing equipment, not in a kitchen.

---

## What to remember

| Era | How | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| 1920s–1980s | ground-up pig and cow pancreas | took tonnes of animals; not quite human |
| 1980s–now | *E. coli* bacteria with the human gene | bacteria can't fold it; needs lots of cleanup |
| Now | **yeast** that folds it *and* hands it over | genuinely elegant |

**The trick everyone misses:** making the molecule is easy. Making it *safe* is the hard part,
and it's the part worth respecting.
