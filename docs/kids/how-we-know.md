# How we found out

← [Back to: Insulin, for kids](README.md)

The other pages tell you *what* is true about insulin. This one is about something more
useful: **how anybody found out.**

That matters, because the world is full of people telling you things confidently. The only
real defense you have is knowing how a claim gets checked — and the insulin story is one of
the best examples there is, partly because it includes the mistakes.

---

## Before 1921, this was a death sentence

If a child got Type 1 diabetes in 1920, they died. Usually within a year, often within months.

Doctors weren't ignorant. They knew the pancreas was involved — in 1889 two researchers in
Strasbourg, **Oskar Minkowski** and **Joseph von Mering**, removed a dog's pancreas for an
unrelated experiment, and a lab assistant noticed flies gathering around the dog's urine. It
was full of sugar. That was the clue: no pancreas → diabetes.

So for thirty years people knew *where* the answer was. They just couldn't get it out.

The only treatment was a **starvation diet** — a few hundred calories a day. It worked, in a
horrible way: it bought some children an extra year by keeping almost no sugar in them at all.
Hospital wards filled up with children who were skeletally thin and slipping into comas, and
parents who sat with them.

That's the situation. Hold onto it, because it's why the next part matters so much.

---

## Why it was so hard

The pancreas has **two completely different jobs**, and they were getting in each other's way.

1. It makes **digestive juice** — powerful stuff that breaks food apart in your intestine.
2. It makes **hormones** — insulin and glucagon, from the little islands.

Here's the trap. Insulin is a **protein**. Digestive juice **destroys proteins** — that is
literally its job.

So every time a researcher mashed up a pancreas to get the insulin out, the digestive juice
spilled out at the same moment and **ate the very thing they were trying to collect.** They
were doing the right experiment and getting nothing, for three decades.

> This is worth noticing: for thirty years, the problem wasn't the *idea*. It was the
> **method**. A good idea with no working method looks exactly like a bad idea.

---

## The attic

In October 1920, a young Canadian surgeon named **Frederick Banting** couldn't sleep. He'd
been reading an article about the pancreas, and he scribbled a note in the middle of the
night.

His idea: if you **tie off the tube** that carries digestive juice out of the pancreas, the
juice-making part would wither away — but the little islands might survive. Then you'd have a
pancreas with the destructive part gone, and you could extract what was left in peace.

Banting wasn't a diabetes expert. He had no lab and no funding. He took the idea to
**J.J.R. Macleod**, a professor at the University of Toronto who *was* an expert. Macleod
thought the odds were poor — but he gave Banting lab space for the summer, ten dogs, and a
student assistant. Then he left for Scotland on holiday.

The student was **Charles Best**, 21 years old. There were two students who wanted the job.
Reportedly, they flipped a coin. Best won.

### The dogs

This part should be told honestly rather than skipped.

Banting and Best worked on dogs through a hot Toronto summer in a room under the roof. They
operated on them, tied off the ducts, waited weeks, then made extracts and tested them on
other dogs whose pancreases had been removed.

**Many of the dogs died.** Some from the surgery, some from infection, some because the early
extracts were impure and dangerous. It was difficult, and both men found it difficult.

One dog became famous. **Dog 33, called Marjorie**, had no pancreas and should have died in
days. On daily injections of the extract she lived for **70 days**. When the experiment ended,
the extract had kept her alive more than ten times longer than she should have lasted.

That was the proof that something real was in the bottle.

Today this work would require ethics committee approval, strict limits on numbers and
suffering, and a serious argument that there was no alternative. Those rules exist *because*
of experiments like this one — people looked at what was done in the past and decided to draw
lines. It's fair to be glad insulin was found and also to think the rules we have now are
better.

## The part where the original idea turns out to be wrong

Here's the twist, and it's the best lesson in the whole story.

The extract worked, but it was filthy — full of impurities that caused fevers and painful
abscesses. It wasn't good enough to give a child.

So Macleod brought in a biochemist, **James Collip**, who was very good at purifying things.
And Collip worked out how to clean it up using alcohol at carefully chosen concentrations.

But in doing so he discovered something awkward: **you didn't need Banting's duct-tying idea
at all.** With the right alcohol method, you could extract insulin from an ordinary whole
pancreas, straight from the slaughterhouse. The elaborate surgery that started the whole
project turned out to be unnecessary.

> **Banting's big idea was, strictly speaking, wrong — and it still led to the cure.**
>
> It got him into the lab. It got him working on the right problem. And once good people were
> working on the right problem with real methods, somebody found the actual answer.
>
> Being wrong in a *useful* direction is one of the most valuable things in science. This is
> why "my idea was interesting" is never the finish line, and why "it worked, so my reasoning
> must have been right" is a trap.

The team, by the way, argued bitterly. Banting and Collip nearly came to blows. Great
discoveries are not usually made by serene geniuses nodding at each other.

## January 1922

**Leonard Thompson** was 14 years old, in Toronto General Hospital, weighing about 65 pounds
and close to death.

On **11 January 1922** he was given the first injection. It was Banting and Best's version,
not yet purified. It barely helped and gave him an abscess.

Collip worked for twelve more days.

On **23 January 1922**, Leonard was given the purified extract. His blood sugar fell almost to
normal. The sugar vanished from his urine. He got stronger. **He lived another thirteen years.**

Then came the moment people still get emotional about. The team went into the wards where
children lay in diabetic comas, families waiting beside them, and injected them one after
another, bed by bed, down the row.

By the time they reached the last child, the first ones were **waking up**.

Parents watched their children come back in front of them.

---

## How we know it wasn't luck

One recovery could be a fluke. A ward full of them is harder to dismiss — but "it looked like
it worked" has fooled people many times. So what made this stick?

- **The dose matched the effect.** More extract, bigger drop in blood sugar. Less, smaller.
  Relationships like that are hard to fake and hard to explain away.
- **It worked in a different species.** Dogs first, then humans. A result that crosses species
  is unlikely to be a local accident.
- **It failed in the predicted way.** Stop the injections, and the patient got worse again,
  exactly as the theory said they would.
- **Other people reproduced it.** Within a year, labs around the world were making insulin and
  getting the same results. **This is the big one.** A result that only works in the hands of
  the person who discovered it is not yet a result.
- **It had a mechanism.** They knew *where* it came from and *why* it should work, not just
  that it seemed to.

That checklist — dose–response, crosses species, fails when predicted, independently
reproduced, has a mechanism — is roughly what scientists still use. It's the thing to ask about
any big claim you meet, including the ones in the strange document this project was built to
check.

**Almost none of the claims in that document pass even two of those five.**

## The dollar

Insulin was going to be worth an enormous amount of money. Everyone knew it.

Banting, Best, and Collip sold the patent to the University of Toronto for **one dollar each.**

They did it deliberately. The idea was that the university would license it to any
manufacturer who could make it properly, so nobody could corner it, and nobody could charge
whatever they liked.

Banting is often quoted as saying insulin belonged to the world and not to him. Whether he
said those exact words is debated. What isn't debated is the dollar, and the dollar says it
clearly enough.

**It is worth knowing what happened afterward.** For decades it worked. Then, slowly, companies
found ways around it — patents not on insulin itself but on the pens, the devices, and the
slightly-modified versions. By the 2010s some Americans were paying hundreds of dollars a
month, and some were taking less than they needed to make a vial last. **People died doing
that.**

After years of public pressure, prices were cut sharply in 2023 and 2024 and out-of-pocket
costs were capped at $35 a month for many people in the United States.

So the story has three parts, and you need all three: **three men gave it away. The system
took it back. People pushed, and got some of it returned.**

That last part only happened because enough people understood what was going on and refused
to accept it. Which is, in the end, what a page like this is for.

---

## Nobel Prize, 1923

The prize went to **Banting and Macleod** — just eighteen months after the discovery, which is
almost unheard of.

Banting was furious that Best wasn't included, and immediately split his prize money with him.
Macleod then split his with Collip.

Four people, one discovery, a lot of arguing, and a cure that has kept tens of millions of
people alive since.
