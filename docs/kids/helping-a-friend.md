# Helping a friend who has diabetes

← [Back to: Insulin, for kids](README.md) · [How we make it](how-we-make-it.md) ·
[How we found out](how-we-know.md)

If someone in your class or your family has Type 1 diabetes, this page is worth knowing by
heart. Most of the time nothing happens and your friend is just a normal kid. But once in a
while something does happen, and a friend who knows what to look for is genuinely useful.

This is short on purpose. Read it twice.

---

## 🚨 The emergency to know: low blood sugar

People with diabetes take insulin to bring their blood sugar **down**. Sometimes it comes down
too far — maybe they took their usual amount but ran around more than usual, or ate less than
they planned. This is called a **hypo** (short for hypoglycemia), and it can come on fast.

### What it looks like

Your friend might be:

- **Shaky or trembling**
- **Sweaty and pale**, even if it isn't hot
- **Confused** — not making sense, or can't follow what you're saying
- **Grumpy, teary, or just not acting like themselves** — this one surprises people. A hypo can
  look like a bad mood or a tantrum.
- **Slurring words** or stumbling, like they're dizzy
- Saying they feel weird, hungry, or far away

> **The rule of thumb:** if your friend with diabetes is suddenly acting *wrong* — confused,
> clumsy, snappish, out of it — assume it's a hypo until a grown-up says otherwise. Don't wait
> to be sure.

### What to do

**1. Get an adult. Right now.** A teacher, a parent, the school nurse, any adult. Shout if you
have to. This is the whole job and it's the most important step — you don't have to fix
anything yourself.

**2. Stay with them.** Don't send them off to the nurse alone. Don't let them wander away. If
they're wobbly, help them sit down.

**3. If they are awake and can swallow, sugar helps fast.** Juice, regular (not diet) soda,
glucose tablets, candy. Lots of kids with diabetes carry something for exactly this — if they
tell you where it is, get it.

### What NOT to do

- ❌ **Don't put food or drink in their mouth if they're sleepy, passed out, or shaking
  (seizing).** They could choke. Just get the adult and stay there.
- ❌ **Don't tell them to walk it off or keep playing.** Exercise makes a hypo worse.
- ❌ **Don't believe them if they say they're fine but they clearly aren't.** A hypo makes it
  hard to think clearly, so people having one often insist nothing's wrong. Get the adult
  anyway. Your friend will not be mad at you later. Genuinely.

## The slower emergency: blood sugar too high

This one builds up over hours instead of minutes, so it's less dramatic — but it's serious.
It's called **DKA**, and it happens when there isn't enough insulin.

Signs: **really thirsty**, going to the bathroom constantly, **stomach ache**, **throwing up**,
breathing fast and deep, acting sleepy or confused, and sometimes **breath that smells sweet or
fruity**, a bit like nail polish remover.

**What to do: tell an adult.** That's it, that's the whole job. DKA needs a hospital, and the
sooner someone knows, the better it goes.

## The other way to be a good friend

Most of being a good friend to someone with diabetes has nothing to do with emergencies.

**Don't make it weird.** Your friend might wear a little plastic thing on their arm (a glucose
sensor) or a small device clipped on them (a pump). They might check their phone a lot — it's
probably showing their blood sugar, not a game. They might need to eat a snack in the middle of
class, or step out for a minute. None of this needs to be commented on.

**Don't police their food.** This matters more than you'd think. People say incredibly annoying
things to kids with diabetes, like *"should you be eating that?"* The answer is: that is between
them, their family, and their doctor, and they have thought about it a lot more than you have.
Someone with Type 1 can eat a cupcake. They adjust their insulin for it.

**Shut down the "you got it from eating too much sugar" thing.** You'd be amazed how often kids
with Type 1 hear this, and it isn't true even a little bit. Type 1 happens when the immune
system attacks the pancreas by mistake. Nobody causes it, and nobody catches it from anybody —
you can't get diabetes from your friend any more than you can get freckles from them. If you
hear someone say otherwise, you now know enough to correct them.

**Ask them, once, what they'd want you to do.** Not in a big serious way. Just: *"hey, if you
ever go low, what do you want me to do?"* Most people appreciate being asked instead of being
treated like glass. Then drop it and go be friends.

## The short version

| Situation | What you do |
|---|---|
| Friend is shaky, sweaty, confused, or acting *wrong* | **Get an adult now.** Stay with them. Sugar if they're awake. |
| Friend is passed out or shaking | **Get an adult now.** Nothing in their mouth. |
| Friend is super thirsty, sick to their stomach, breathing hard | **Tell an adult.** |
| Normal Tuesday | Be normal. They're just your friend. |

**Call for help:** 911 (US) · 999 (UK) · 112 (EU)
