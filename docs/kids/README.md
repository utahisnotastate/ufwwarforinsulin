## Your body runs on sugar

Not candy sugar, exactly. When you eat *anything* — bread, apples, pasta, chicken — your body
breaks it down into a simple fuel called **glucose**. Glucose is a kind of sugar, and it travels
around your body in your blood like fuel in a pipeline.

Every single part of you runs on it. Your legs when you run. Your heart. Your brain right now,
reading this. Your brain is a serious fuel hog — it uses about a fifth of all the energy you
have, even though it's only about 2% of your weight.

So the fuel is in your blood. But here's the problem: **the fuel has to get out of your blood
and into your cells**, which is where it actually gets used. Your cells are like tiny rooms, and
they have doors. And the doors are locked.

## Insulin is the key

Insulin is a tiny thing your body makes — way too small to see, even with a regular microscope.
It's made in your **pancreas**, which is a flat, lumpy organ tucked behind your stomach.

When you eat, your blood fills with glucose. Your pancreas notices and sends out insulin.
Insulin travels to your cells, fits into a special spot on the outside of each one, and the
doors open. Glucose rushes in. Your cells get fed. The level of sugar in your blood goes back
down to normal.

Then you eat again, and the whole thing happens again. Your pancreas does this all day, every
day, your entire life, without you ever thinking about it. It is extremely good at its job.

> **The big idea:** insulin doesn't *make* energy. It doesn't *give* you energy. It **unlocks
> the door** so the energy you already ate can get where it's going.

## What diabetes is

Diabetes is what we call it when that system stops working. There are two main kinds, and they
are really quite different from each other.

### Type 1 — the key-makers are gone

In Type 1 diabetes, the body's own defense system gets confused. Normally your immune system
attacks germs. But in Type 1, it makes a mistake and attacks the cells in the pancreas that
make insulin — the **beta cells** — until there aren't any left.

No beta cells, no insulin. No insulin, no key. The glucose piles up in the blood with nowhere to
go, and the cells go hungry *even though the body is full of fuel*. It's like a locked pantry
during a feast.

Two things that are really important and that lots of people get wrong:

- **It is not caused by eating too much sugar.** It isn't caused by anything the person did.
- **It usually starts in kids and teenagers**, though adults can get it too.

People with Type 1 **must** take insulin — with a tiny needle, a pen, or a little pump they
wear. They're not choosing to. There is no other option, and there isn't a food or an exercise
that replaces it. That's just how it is right now, and it's why the invention of insulin was
such an enormous deal.

### Type 2 — the rooms are already full

Type 2 is a different puzzle, and the best way to picture it comes from a story about a
freight train.

> Imagine a train car that is already packed completely full of heavy crates. The doors are
> bulging. Now the yard boss shows up with ten more crates and tries to turn the key. The key
> works fine! But the door won't budge, because the car is stuffed.
>
> So the boss decides the key must be broken, and brings a giant hammer.

In Type 2 diabetes, the key (insulin) is usually still there — sometimes there's *extra*. But
the cells have gotten so full of stored fuel, especially fat stored in the wrong places, that
they stop opening their doors. Scientists call this **insulin resistance**. The cell isn't
broken. It's full, and it's protecting itself.

That's why Type 2 is treated differently — a lot of the work is about helping cells empty out
and become able to listen again, through food, movement, and medicines that work in several
different ways.

*(That freight car picture comes from the source material this project is built on. It's the
best explanation in it, and it's genuinely good science.)*

## The part of the story that should make you proud of people

Before 1921, if a kid got Type 1 diabetes, they died. Usually within a year. Doctors could do
almost nothing. The only "treatment" was a starvation diet that bought a few extra months of
being miserable. Hospital wards had rooms full of children in comas, with parents waiting.

Then in a hot attic laboratory in Toronto, a surgeon named **Frederick Banting**, a student
named **Charles Best**, a professor named **J.J.R. Macleod**, and a biochemist named **James
Collip** worked out how to get insulin out of animal pancreases and make it pure enough to use.

In January 1922 they gave it to a 14-year-old boy named **Leonard Thompson**, who was dying. He
got better.

Then something amazing happened. Doctors went into those wards full of dying children and
injected them one after another. By the time they reached the last bed, the first children were
*waking up*. Parents watched their kids come back.

And here's the part that matters for the rest of this project: the discoverers **sold the patent
to the University of Toronto for one dollar each.** They did it on purpose. They thought insulin
was too important to own.

## What to remember

| | |
|---|---|
| **Glucose** | the fuel in your blood, from all food |
| **Insulin** | the key that lets fuel into your cells |
| **Pancreas** | the organ that makes insulin |
| **Type 1** | no key-makers left — must take insulin, not anyone's fault |
| **Type 2** | the cells are too full to open — treated differently |

**One more thing, and it's the most important thing on this page.** You may run into stories
online about special machines, magnets, frequencies, or diets that replace insulin for people
with Type 1. They are not true, and believing them is dangerous. A person with Type 1 who stops
taking insulin gets very sick within a day or two. If you ever hear someone say that, tell a
grown-up.
